Assignment 2: Who Do We Know the Least About?

Measuring uncertainty in American Community Survey data

Author

Chloe Alimurong

Published

September 7, 2026

Setup

library(tidyverse)
library(tidycensus)

The question

In this analysis, I look at median home values across counties in Maryland, and ask which communities we measure most and least reliably. I am interested in seeing the trends over time, especially before and after COVID and counties with more urban areas. Or, if there’s a trend amongst demographics such as having a Bachelors degree. I wonder if there’s data in specific communities that’s least reliable and if I can find potential explanations in those discrepancies.

Choosing what to measure

my_state <- "MD"        
my_variable <- "B25077_001"     # Median home value

Pulling the data

county_data <- get_acs(
  geography = "county",
  variables = my_variable,
  state = my_state,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5"
)

How big is the margin of error?

county_data <- county_data %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = moe / estimate * 100)

The county with the largest margin of error relative to its estimate is Somerset county at 9.1% MOE, making it the least reliable county in MD for my variable.

Sorting counties into reliability categories

county_data <- county_data %>%
  mutate(reliability = case_when(
    moe_pct < 1  ~ "High confidence",
    moe_pct < 5 ~ "Moderate",
    TRUE         ~ "Low confidence"
  ))

count(county_data, reliability)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  reliability         n
  <chr>           <int>
1 High confidence     4
2 Low confidence      2
3 Moderate           18

4 counties in MD fall into the ‘High’ confidence category. 18 fall into the ‘Moderate’ confidence category. 2 fall into the ‘Low’ confidence category. I rearranged the threshold to be 1% for high confidence and 5% for moderate since my state is small and there are counties with an even smaller population so they mostly fell into high confidence.

Is the margin of error bigger in small places?

county_wide <- get_acs(
  geography = "county",
  variables = c(myvar = my_variable,
                pop   = "B01003_001"),
  state = my_state,
  year = 2023,
  survey = "acs5",
  output = "wide"
)

head(county_wide)
# A tibble: 6 × 6
  GEOID NAME                          myvarE myvarM   popE  popM
  <chr> <chr>                          <dbl>  <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>
1 24001 Allegany County, Maryland     149200   5832  67762    NA
2 24003 Anne Arundel County, Maryland 450300   4485 590936    NA
3 24005 Baltimore County, Maryland    330000   3148 849586    NA
4 24009 Calvert County, Maryland      440200   8554  93791    NA
5 24011 Caroline County, Maryland     272400   7506  33406    NA
6 24013 Carroll County, Maryland      406400   7074 174318    NA
county_wide <- county_wide %>%
  mutate(moe_pct = myvarM / myvarE * 100) %>%
  mutate(reliability = case_when(
    moe_pct < 1  ~ "High confidence",
    moe_pct < 5 ~ "Moderate",
    TRUE         ~ "Low confidence"
  ))
county_wide %>%
  group_by(reliability) %>%
  summarize(
    n_counties = n(),
    avg_population = mean(popE)
  )
# A tibble: 3 × 3
  reliability     n_counties avg_population
  <chr>                <int>          <dbl>
1 High confidence          4        863423 
2 Low confidence           2         21975 
3 Moderate                18        148505.

The average population of high-confidence counties is 863,423 people. The low-confidence counties has an average of 21,975 people while the moderate averages at 148,506 people. This pattern tells us that the greater the average population, the greater likelihood of a higher margin of error is for the median home value variable.

What this means for policy

As a MD resident, this makes sense that the counties we know the least about/have less accurate information on, are the counties with smaller populations. Somerset county has the highest MOE at 9.1%, I don’t really know anyone from there, nor Kent (5.8%) or Garrett (4.7%) county, the top 2 and 3 for highest MOE. These are counties with wild horses, other wildlife, and beaches. The counties we pass through on our way to Ocean City where you can’t really see houses. Somerset county, MD is one of the few counties in the Northeastern-ish US that only recently needed fiber (internet cable) built to. Basically, not a lot of people live there. Not enough to have a significant statistics on median home value. This is likely because of sampling variability in results we can’t account for in small counties. ACS is a survey of a sample so bigger counties like Baltimore and Montgomery would receive more responses to the survey than a small one like Somerset. There could be a really expensive or really cheap house that could be skewing the data that adds to statistical uncertainty in a small county. For state agencies studying the variable, median home value, smaller counties would be at a disadvantage. For example, if a state agency wants to allocate funding such as decreasing or increasing the construction of affordable housing units or even discounting/increasing APR for homeowner loans, residents of these smaller counties that need funding would be left out since the data likely misrepresented them or left them out completely. For example, Somerset county’s estimated median home value is $164,300, for those looking to move to Somerset county, they wouldn’t receive much assistance as the predicted value is lower than most counties in MD.
